# Brief description

A nephelometer is an optical analytical instrument that measures the concentration of suspended colloidal particles in a gas or liquid by quantifying the intensity of light scattered at a designated angle (typically 90 degrees) to the incident light beam. Conceptualized following John Tyndall's 1869 discoveries on light scattering by aerosols and colloidal suspensions (the Tyndall effect) and formulated into quantitative instruments by Theodore Richards and others in the 1890s, the nephelometer differs fundamentally from a turbidimeter: while turbidimeters measure the reduction of light passing directly through a fluid, nephelometers detect light scattered sideways against a pitch-black background. This geometry provides exceptional sensitivity, enabling detection of minute trace suspensions in ultra-clean drinking water, atmospheric aerosols, and immunological precipitations.

## Use / Function

- **High-Purity Water Quality Monitoring:** Measuring extremely low turbidity in potable water (below 1 NTU) to detect microscopic cysts, bacteria, and fine silt that escape standard visual detection.
- **Atmospheric Aerosol & Smoke Detection:** Quantifying airborne dust, soot, aerosol concentrations, and smoke density in mine shafts and cleanrooms.
- **Biochemical & Clinical Immunology:** Measuring antigen-antibody precipitation kinetics, protein concentrations, and bacterial culture growth rates without lengthy incubation.
- **Colloidal Chemical Synthesis:** Monitoring crystallization initiation, latex emulsion polymerization, and colloid flocculation in industrial chemical synthesis.

## Operating principle

1. **Collimated Beam Projection:** A concentrated, parallel beam of light (from an incandescent lamp, arc light, or daylight slit) passes through a light aperture into a sample cell.
2. **Tyndall Light Scattering:** When the incident photons strike suspended colloidal particles whose diameters are comparable to or slightly larger than the wavelength of light, light is scattered in all directions (Tyndall and Rayleigh-Debye scattering).
3. **Right-Angle Detection Geometry:** An optical detector (visual comparison eyepiece, photodiode, or photomultiplier tube) is positioned at a right angle (90°) relative to the incoming illumination axis.
4. **Dark-Field Contrast:** Because the direct primary beam passes through the cell and is absorbed by a light trap (such as a Wood's horn or blackened chamber), only photons scattered sideways by suspended particles reach the detector against a pitch-black background.
5. **Linear Intensity Proportionality:** At low particle concentrations, the intensity of scattered light ($I_s$) is directly proportional to particle number and mass concentration ($I_s \propto C$), yielding linear readings in Nephelometric Turbidity Units (NTU).

## How to create it

1. **Construct the Enclosed Housing:** Fabricate a light-tight rectangular enclosure from sheet brass, aluminum, or wood lined with light-absorbing velvet or painted with ultra-matte black paint.
2. **Assemble Collimating Optics:** Install a stable light source at one end, aligning a double-convex condensing glass lens and an adjustable iris diaphragm to project a sharp, parallel light beam horizontally across the chamber.
3. **Build the Cuvette Stage:** Machine a square central holder to secure an optical-grade cylindrical or rectangular glass cuvette centered precisely within the horizontal light axis.
4. **Install Light Traps:** Place a light-trap horn (a curved, blackened glass or metal cone) directly opposite the light source along the primary beam axis to completely absorb unscattered light without back-reflections.
5. **Construct the 90-Degree Viewing Port:** Mount an eyepiece tube or photodetector port perpendicular (at 90°) to the incident beam, equipped with an adjustable slit and comparison prism or half-silvered mirror.
6. **Incorporate Calibration Standards:** Calibrate the instrument using stable formazin polymer suspensions or standardized ground silica standards to establish zero and reference NTU points.

## Materials needed

- **Essential:** Optical-quality **[Glass](/en/materials/glass)** for cuvettes, collimating lenses, and comparison prisms; rigid **[Brass](/en/materials/brass)** or **[Metal](/en/materials/metal)** for the optical housing, apertures, and lens cells; seasoned **[Wood](/en/materials/wood)** for outer chassis insulation; and pure distilled **[Water](/en/materials/water)** for blank baseline calibrations.
- **Tools:** Lathe, glass-cutting tools, lens holder jigs, aperture punches, matte black paint, optical bench alignment rail, standardized calibration suspensions (formazin or barium sulfate).
- **Substitutes:** Visual comparison nephelometers can use adjustable optical wedges or dual matched cuvettes compared against a standard; modern versions replace visual eyepieces with silicon phototransistors or photocells.

## Variants and improvements

- **Richards Nephelometer:** The foundational laboratory visual instrument using dual immersion tubes and variable optical slits to compare scattered light against known reference standards.
- **Integrating Nephelometer:** Measures light scattered over a wide angular range (typically 7° to 170°) using a diffuse light source and integrating cylinder; standard in atmospheric and aerosol research.
- **Laser Nephelometer:** Utilizes a coherent laser diode as the light source, offering high monochromatic intensity and sensitivity to sub-micron colloidal particles.
- **Ratio Nephelometer:** Incorporates both 90° scattered light and transmitted light detectors to compensate for fluid sample color and lamp intensity fluctuations.

## Limits and risks

- **Optical Cuvette Scratches:** Scratches, imperfections, or dust on the glass cuvette surfaces scatter light, producing significant false background signals in low-turbidity samples.
- **Stray Light Reflections:** Imperfect interior blackening or unabsorbed direct beam reflection will cause stray photons to enter the 90° detector, destroying low-end measurement accuracy.
- **Particle Size Dependency:** Scattered light intensity varies dramatically with particle diameter and refractive index ratio, meaning calibration in mass per volume requires knowledge of particle composition.
