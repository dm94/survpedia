# Brief description

A turbidimeter is an optical analytical instrument used to measure the cloudiness, haziness, or turbidity of a liquid caused by large numbers of individual suspended insoluble particles. First developed in standardized form by Whipple and Jackson in 1900 (the Jackson Candle Turbidimeter), the device quantifies optical transmission or light attenuation through a fluid column. In post-collapse recovery, water sanitation, brewing, and chemical processing, a turbidimeter is essential to determine whether drinking water has been sufficiently filtered, monitor coagulation and flocculation, and verify that fluid is free of pathogen-harboring suspended solids.

## Use / Function

- **Potable Water Quality Assurance:** Assessing drinking water clarity before and after slow sand filtration or chemical coagulation to ensure compliance with sanitary standards.
- **Filtration Plant Monitoring:** Detecting filter breakthrough, bed exhaustion, or channel formation in municipal or community water filtration beds.
- **Beverage Clarification:** Monitoring clarity and haze during beer brewing, winemaking, vinegar production, and fruit juice pressing.
- **Wastewater & Settling Control:** Measuring suspended solids settling rates in sedimentation tanks, clarifiers, and environmental runoff.

## Operating principle

1. **Light Extinction & Attenuation:** When a collimated or diffuse light beam travels through a liquid containing suspended particulate matter, photons are scattered and absorbed (attenuated) rather than transmitted straight through.
2. **Path Length Proportionality:** According to the Beer-Lambert law principles applied to particulate suspensions, the total optical extinction increases with both particle concentration and the physical depth of the fluid column.
3. **Visual Extinction Threshold:** In visual extinction turbidimeters (such as the Jackson candle turbidimeter), an observer looks vertically down through a tall, calibrated glass column at a standardized candle flame placed directly beneath the flat glass base.
4. **Endpoint Determination:** Liquid is poured into the tube until the suspended particles scatter enough light that the distinct flame image disappears, blending into a uniform diffuse glow.
5. **Scale Graduation:** The liquid depth at the exact moment of flame extinction is inversely proportional to turbidity; the column is graduated directly in Jackson Turbidity Units (JTU) or parts per million (ppm) silica equivalents.

## How to create it

1. **Construct the Support Chassis:** Build a sturdy vertical bench chassis or tripod stand from wood or sheet metal, incorporating an enclosed lower combustion chamber and an open top viewing bracket.
2. **Mount the Standard Light Source:** Secure a spring-loaded candle holder at the base designed to keep the tip of a standard spermaceti, beeswax, or paraffin candle at a constant fixed distance (typically 3 inches / 7.6 cm) below the tube support plate.
3. **Fabricate the Calibrated Glass Tube:** Select a straight, clear, optical-quality glass cylinder (typically 2.5 cm inside diameter and 25 to 75 cm length) with a polished flat optical glass bottom disc fused without distortion.
4. **Graduate the Height Scale:** Etch or mark a calibrated inverse scale along the outer wall of the glass tube, correlating liquid depth in centimeters to turbidity units calibrated with standardized diatomaceous earth or kaolin clay suspensions.
5. **Install Stray Light Baffles:** Paint the interior of the candle chamber and tube collar with non-reflective matte black paint to prevent internal reflections and ambient glare from disturbing the extinction reading.
6. **Integrate Drain and Fill Tube:** Fit a rubber or glass siphon tube with a pinch clamp to allow slow, bubble-free addition and draining of sample liquid while observing the flame from above.

## Materials needed

- **Essential:** Clear optical **[Glass](/en/materials/glass)** for the flat-bottomed extinction tube; durable **[Brass](/en/materials/brass)** or **[Metal](/en/materials/metal)** for the candle carrier and tube chassis; seasoned **[Wood](/en/materials/wood)** for stable bench mounting; and pure **[Water](/en/materials/water)** for standard suspension calibration.
- **Tools:** Glass cutter, torch or sealing burner, graduated cylinder, precision ruler, matte black paint, standardized candles or flame source.
- **Substitutes:** A stable battery-powered incandescent bulb with a small aperture can substitute for a standard candle; standardized Secchi disks lowered into open water provide an analogous visual extinction depth measurement.

## Variants and improvements

- **Jackson Candle Turbidimeter (JCT):** The historical benchmark instrument for measuring turbidity above 25 JTU using a standardized burning candle beneath a flat-bottomed tube.
- **Secchi Disk:** A weighted circular plate painted with alternating black and white quadrants, lowered by rope into lakes, wells, or reservoirs to measure open-water visual clarity depth.
- **Parr Turbidimeter:** An enclosed visual comparator using an electric filament lamp beneath a variable-depth plunger tube.
- **Ratio Turbidimeter:** An advanced electro-optical instrument combining transmitted light photodetectors with 90-degree scattered light detectors to compensate for liquid color absorption.

## Limits and risks

- **Lower Sensitivity Boundary:** Visual extinction turbidimeters cannot reliably measure low turbidity below approximately 25 JTU, making them unsuitable for ultra-pure water without pre-concentration.
- **Observer Fatigue & Subjectivity:** Human eye sensitivity varies between observers and diminishes with prolonged exposure to diffuse glare, introducing observational bias.
- **Color Interference:** Dissolved color in the liquid (such as tea-colored tannins or humic acids) absorbs light without scattering, falsely increasing apparent turbidity readings.
