# Brief description

A pyrheliometer is a specialized radiometer and meteorological instrument designed to measure direct beam solar irradiance at normal incidence. Invented in 1837 by French physicist Claude Pouillet and subsequently refined by Knut Ångström and Charles Greeley Abbot, the pyrheliometer provided the first empirical, quantitative measurements of the total thermal power radiating from the Sun onto the Earth's surface. By tracking the direct heating rate of a collimated black absorber target, the pyrheliometer enabled scientists to calculate the solar constant, evaluate atmospheric transparency, and design modern concentrated solar thermal and photovoltaic energy systems.

## Use / Function

- **Solar Constant Determination:** Calculating the total extraterrestrial solar radiation power entering Earth's atmosphere per unit area.
- **Direct Beam Solar Irradiance Measurement:** Measuring direct solar thermal power ($\text{W/m}^2$) without interference from diffuse diffuse scattered sky radiation.
- **Atmospheric Turbidity and Attenuation Studies:** Monitoring dust, smoke, aerosols, and moisture content in the atmospheric column through solar beam extinction.
- **Solar Energy Engineering:** Assessing site viability and tracking efficiency for concentrated solar collectors, parabolic concentrators, and solar furnaces.

## Operating principle

1. **Collimated Optical Geometry:** A narrow cylindrical tube with internal diaphragms limits the field of view strictly to the solar disc and a tiny circumsolar halo (typically a 5-degree acceptance angle), shielding the sensor from scattered diffuse sky radiation.
2. **Blackbody Radiation Absorption:** Direct sunlight strikes a blackened absorber target (coated with lampblack or platinum black) with an absorption coefficient near 0.98, converting all incident radiant wavelengths directly into thermal energy.
3. **Calorimetric Heat Absorption:** In Pouillet's original water pyrheliometer, the blackened silver vessel contained a known mass of water with an immersed precision thermometer; the temperature rise per minute in full sunlight directly reflected the incoming thermal power ($Q = m c \Delta T$).
4. **Electrical Substitution Balance (Ångström Principle):** In modern electrical pyrheliometers, two identical thin manganin strips are used. One strip absorbs solar radiation while the other is heated by an adjustable electrical current; when thermocouple junctions indicate identical temperatures, the radiant solar flux precisely equals the measured electrical power ($I^2 R$).
5. **Normal Incidence Alignment:** The instrument is mounted on an equatorial mount or two-axis heliostat mechanism to continually point directly at the center of the Sun throughout the day.

## How to create it

1. **Build the Collimating Tube:** Construct a rigid brass or aluminum tube of suitable length (approx. 20 to 30 cm) fitted with internal blackened aperture rings to collimate incoming light and baffle internal reflections.
2. **Fabricate the Calorimeter Target:** Form a shallow, thin-walled silver or copper vessel with a flat blackened face, mounted at the bottom of the tube inside a thermally insulated jacket (using felt or wood).
3. **Fill with Calorimetric Fluid:** Fill the vessel with a precisely weighed volume of distilled water, ensuring no air bubbles remain inside.
4. **Mount Precision Thermometer:** Insert a sensitive, finely graduated mercury-in-glass thermometer through a sealed packing gland into the fluid chamber, bending or positioning the stem for clear external viewing.
5. **Install Shutter and Sight Alignment:** Fit an insulated flip-open lens cap or shutter over the front aperture, and mount a pinhole-and-target sight along the exterior tube to align the device with the solar beam.
6. **Execute Calibration Sequence:** Mount the device on a steady tripod; shield from direct sun and record ambient temperature drift for 5 minutes; uncap the shutter and expose to direct sunlight for 5 minutes recording the heating rate; close the shutter and record cooling rate for 5 minutes to compensate for ambient heat loss.

## Materials needed

- **Essential:** Thermally conductive **[Metal](/en/materials/metal)** (brass, copper, silver) for the collimating tube, apertures, and calorimeter vessel; clear **[Glass](/en/materials/glass)** for the precision thermometer and optics; pure **[Water](/en/materials/water)** as the standard calorimetric heat absorption mass; and well-seasoned **[Wood](/en/materials/wood)** for mounting bases and thermal insulation.
- **Tools:** Metal lathe, precision thermometer, analytical balance, soldering iron, black matte paint (lampblack), stopwatch, equatorial tracking tripod.
- **Substitutes:** Modern pyrheliometers substitute liquid water calorimeters with bimetallic strips, differential thermopile detectors, or electrical substitution manganin ribbons.

## Variants and improvements

- **Pouillet's Water Pyrheliometer:** The foundational 1837 design featuring a blackened flat silver box filled with water and an embedded sensitive thermometer.
- **Ångström Electrical Compensation Pyrheliometer:** Employs twin blackened manganin strips and thermocouples, establishing the absolute radiometric reference standard by matching solar heat with electrical Joule heating.
- **Silver-Disk Pyrheliometer (Abbot):** Replaced the liquid water vessel with a blackened copper-encased pure silver disk, significantly reducing fluid convection errors in Smithsonian solar surveys.
- **Cavity Pyrheliometer:** Employs an inverted conical blackened cavity sensor approximating a true blackbody receiver, operating on automated solar tracking mounts for satellite and weather station networks.

## Limits and risks

- **Atmospheric Scintillation and Cloud Drift:** Passing clouds, thin cirrus veils, or industrial smoke plumes disrupt direct beam constancy, rendering instantaneous measurements inaccurate.
- **Tracking Misalignment:** If the collimation tube deviates more than a fraction of a degree off the solar disc axis, the sensor will be partially shadowed, yielding falsely low solar irradiance.
- **Convective Heat Losses:** High ambient wind cooling the exterior tube requires mathematical thermal loss compensation or vacuum isolation to prevent underestimating solar thermal flux.
