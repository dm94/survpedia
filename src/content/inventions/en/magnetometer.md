# Brief description

A magnetometer is a scientific instrument designed to measure the strength, direction, and relative variations of magnetic fields, including Earth's geomagnetic field. First developed quantitatively by Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1832, deflection and vibration magnetometers enabled early physicists to map geomagnetic forces in absolute physical units and paved the way for mineral exploration, navigation, and geophysics.

## Use / Function

- **Geomagnetic Surveying:** Mapping local variations, declinations, and inclinations in Earth's geomagnetic field.
- **Mineral & Ore Exploration:** Locating subterranean iron deposits and magnetic minerals through localized magnetic field anomalies.
- **Navigation & Calibration:** Providing accurate calibration for navigational compasses and detecting magnetic interference from ships and metallic structures.
- **Geophysical Monitoring:** Tracking solar geomagnetic storms, auroral activity, and tectonic stress changes.

## Operating principle

1. **Magnetic Alignment:** A small calibrated bar magnet (or magnetized needle) is suspended horizontally by an unspun silk fiber or fine wire within a draft-free chamber.
2. **Field Vector Interaction:** In equilibrium, the needle aligns itself along the local horizontal component of Earth's magnetic field ($H$).
3. **Deflection Balance:** When a secondary reference bar magnet of known magnetic moment is placed perpendicular to the needle at a measured distance, it exerts an opposing torque. The needle deflects to an angle ($\theta$) where the two torques balance: $\frac{M}{H} = \frac{r^3}{2} \tan\theta$.
4. **Vibration Period Determination:** By removing the deflecting magnet and timing the oscillation frequency of the suspended magnet, the product $M \cdot H$ is determined. Combining deflection and oscillation equations allows calculating the absolute strength of Earth's field ($H$) independent of the magnet's decay.
5. **Optical Angle Reading:** A tiny mirror affixed to the suspended magnet reflects a light beam onto a graduated scale, allowing microscopic deflection readings without friction.

## How to create it

1. **Fabricate Non-Magnetic Base:** Construct a sturdy, leveled platform and support arm entirely out of seasoned wood or brass to prevent stray ferromagnetic interference.
2. **Build Isolation Chamber:** Assemble a wooden housing with glass viewing windows to shield the suspended element from air currents and ambient drafts.
3. **Suspend Magnetic Element:** Hang a small, uniformly magnetized needle or bar magnet using an unspun silk filament or fine silver wire from an upper torsion head.
4. **Attach Optical Pointer:** Affix a miniature silvered glass mirror to the suspension stirrup just above the magnet.
5. **Install Calibrated Rails:** Mount graduated wooden or brass arms extending east and west from the center along which auxiliary magnets can be placed at precise distances.
6. **Set Up Reading Scale:** Position a distant telescope or collimated light beam and curved millimeter scale to read angular deflections via optical reflection.

## Materials needed

- **Essential:** Non-ferrous **[Brass](/en/materials/brass)** fittings and screws, magnetized **[Magnetite](/en/materials/magnetite)** or hardened steel needle, seasoned **[Wood](/en/materials/wood)** for the frame, clear **[Glass](/en/materials/glass)** panels, **[Silver](/en/materials/silver)** mirror fragment.
- **Tools:** Jewelers' screwdrivers, graduated rules, leveling plumb-line, unspun silk thread, stopwatch.
- **Substitutes:** Aluminum or bronze can replace brass; copper wire or quartz fiber can serve as the suspension line.

## Variants and improvements

- **Gauss Deflection Magnetometer:** Employs a suspended magnet and a graduated deflection bar to compute absolute geomagnetic intensity.
- **Bifilar Magnetometer:** Uses two parallel suspension threads to measure minute diurnal variations in the horizontal geomagnetic component.
- **Fluxgate Magnetometer:** An electrical evolution utilizing high-permeability magnetic cores wound with excitation coils to measure vector field components electronically.
- **Proton Precession Magnetometer:** A modern quantum instrument utilizing nuclear magnetic resonance of protons in a liquid hydrocarbon to measure total magnetic intensity.

## Limits and risks

- **Ferromagnetic Contamination:** Even minute traces of iron, steel tools, or nearby nails will distort measurements; all components must be strictly non-magnetic.
- **Drafts and Temperature Fluctuations:** Air currents and temperature-induced changes in magnetic dipole strength can introduce measurement errors.
- **Geomagnetic Disturbance:** Solar flares and lightning discharges cause rapid geomagnetic fluctuations that complicate baseline calibration.
