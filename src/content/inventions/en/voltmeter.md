# Brief description

A voltmeter is an electrical measuring instrument designed to quantify the electric potential difference, electromotive force, or voltage between two points in an electrical circuit. Evolving from early high-resistance galvanometers through the innovations of Edward Weston in 1888, the practical voltmeter became the indispensable companion to the ammeter. In technical reconstruction, electrical generation, and electronics maintenance, a voltmeter is critical for setting generator excitation, monitoring battery state-of-charge, verifying insulation integrity, and diagnosing circuit faults without drawing significant power from the measured system.

## Use / Function

- **Voltage Level Verification:** Checking steady output voltage across power distribution buses, dynamos, and alternators.
- **Battery State Monitoring:** Measuring open-circuit and loaded terminal voltages to evaluate battery discharge level and health.
- **Power Supply Regulation:** Calibrating voltage regulators, transformer taps, and excitation rheostats.
- **Troubleshooting Circuit Breaks:** Tracing voltage drops across components, switches, fuses, and connections to pinpoint open circuits or high-resistance faults.

## Operating principle

1. **Electromechanical Torque:** Similar to the D'Arsonval moving-coil movement, current passing through a fine coil in a uniform radial magnetic field experiences a rotational torque proportional to the current.
2. **Current Proportionality to Voltage:** According to Ohm's law ($I = V / R$), the current that flows through an electrical path of fixed resistance is directly proportional to the voltage applied across its terminals.
3. **High-Resistance Series Multiplier:** To measure voltage across two points without loading the circuit down, a precision high-value resistor (multiplier resistor) is wired in series with the sensitive moving coil.
4. **Parallel Circuit Connection:** The voltmeter is connected in parallel across the two terminals or components being probed; its very high total impedance ensures that only a negligible current is diverted from the active circuit.
5. **Linear Voltage Scale:** Because the deflection current is strictly linear with respect to applied voltage, the meter dial can be graduated directly in volts across an evenly spaced arc.

## How to create it

1. **Build the Insulated Enclosure:** Construct a protective housing from seasoned non-conductive **[Wood](/en/materials/wood)**, fitted with an anti-reflective viewing glass and mounting lugs.
2. **Mount the Permanent Magnet Assembly:** Secure a high-permeability magnetized **[Iron](/en/materials/iron)** or steel magnet with soft iron pole pieces, surrounding a central stationary soft iron cylinder to form a narrow radial air gap.
3. **Construct the Sensitive Moving Coil:** Wind hundreds of turns of ultra-fine insulated **[Copper](/en/materials/copper)** wire around an ultra-lightweight aluminum former, pivoted on jewel cups or hardened steel pivots.
4. **Fit Control Springs and Balance Pointer:** Attach dual opposed hairsprings providing electrical contact and counter-torque; attach a lightweight knife-edge pointer with counterweights.
5. **Wound and Calibrate the Multiplier Resistor:** Wind a high-resistance spool of fine, high-resistivity wire (such as nichrome, manganin, or constantan) or assemble carbon resistors totaling tens of thousands of ohms; connect this multiplier strictly in series with the coil.
6. **Integrate Insulated Terminals:** Install high-dielectric **[Brass](/en/materials/brass)** binding posts insulated from the chassis with vulcanized fiber or bone washers, clearly marked for polarity (+ and -).

## Materials needed

- **Essential:** Fine insulated **[Copper](/en/materials/copper)** and **[Wire](/en/materials/wire)** for the sensitive coil and high-resistance multiplier spool; magnetized **[Iron](/en/materials/iron)** for the permanent field magnet; precision **[Brass](/en/materials/brass)** for terminals and mechanical pivots; and dry **[Wood](/en/materials/wood)** or bakelite for the insulating chassis.
- **Tools:** Wire winding jig, micro-soldering iron, jeweler's loupe, precision tweezers, calibration voltage reference (such as a Daniell or Clark standard cell).
- **Substitutes:** An electrostatic electrometer or a gold-leaf electroscope calibrated against known potentials can substitute for an electromagnetic moving-coil voltmeter in high-voltage static applications.

## Variants and improvements

- **Moving-Coil Voltmeter (DC):** Highly accurate, linearly scaled instrument for direct current measurements with high internal sensitivity.
- **Electrostatic Voltmeter:** Operates by electrostatic attraction between charged plates; draws zero direct current, making it ideal for ultra-high voltage networks.
- **Moving-Iron Voltmeter:** Employs stationary coils and repulsive iron vanes; directly capable of reading both AC and DC root-mean-square voltages.
- **Multi-Range Voltmeter:** Features multiple switched series multiplier resistors selectable via a rotary knob or multiple binding posts (e.g., 3V, 15V, 150V, 300V).

## Limits and risks

- **Circuit Loading Effect:** If the voltmeter's internal resistance is not sufficiently high compared to the circuit under test, it will draw appreciable current, falsely lowering the measured voltage reading.
- **High Voltage Breakdown:** Applying voltages beyond the insulation rating can arc across the multiplier resistor or coil winding, permanently destroying the meter and posing shock hazards.
- **Accidental Series Connection:** While connecting a voltmeter across a power source is safe, connecting it in series with heavy loads will severely restrict current flow and prevent the load from operating.
