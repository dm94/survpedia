# Brief description

A barograph is a self-registering meteorological instrument that continuously records atmospheric pressure fluctuations over time onto a revolving paper chart. Invented in the nineteenth century using bellows-like aneroid capsules (or siphon mercury columns) connected to a clockwork drum, the barograph transformed weather forecasting by providing barometric trend histories—allowing sailors, farmers, and scientists to anticipate gale-force storms, tropical cyclones, and atmospheric fronts hours before their arrival.

## Use / Function

- **Continuous Barometric Trend Tracking:** Providing a graphic record of rising or falling atmospheric pressure over days or weeks.
- **Storm and Front Forecasting:** Detecting sudden barometric drops that reliably signal imminent squalls, blizzards, hurricanes, and severe convective storms.
- **Marine Navigation:** Monitoring micro-climatic pressure shifts aboard ships to avoid sailing directly into storm centers.
- **Altitude and Flight Recording:** Serving as a micro-barograph to chart flight profiles, balloon ascents, and mountain pass elevations.

## Operating principle

1. **Pressure Detection:** An evacuated, corrugated metallic capsule (Vidie aneroid cell) contracts as surrounding atmospheric pressure increases and expands as pressure decreases.
2. **Kinematic Amplification:** An internal steel spring balances the vacuum force, while an intricate series of brass levers, bell cranks, and pivot links amplifies the subtle micron-scale displacement of the capsule stack.
3. **Thermal Compensation:** A bimetallic link or controlled residual gas volume inside the capsules compensates for thermal expansion and contraction of the mechanical linkages.
4. **Graphic Trace Recording:** An inked pen nib or capillary stylus mounted at the end of a long, counterbalanced aluminum or brass pointer contacts a cylindrical drum.
5. **Clockwork Rotation:** An internal mechanical clockwork escapement rotates the recording drum precisely once every 24 hours or seven days, producing a continuous pressure graph (barogram) on graduated grid paper.

## How to create it

1. **Construct Aneroid Capsule Stack:** Solder together several thin, corrugate-stamped phosphor bronze or beryllium copper disc diaphragms, evacuate them with a vacuum pump, and seal them with solder around an internal leaf spring.
2. **Build the Mainframe:** Cast and machine a heavy rectangular brass baseplate with a protective glass-paneled wooden or brass dust cover.
3. **Assemble Amplifying Linkage:** Fabricate frictionless brass pivots, jewel or needle bearings, and a balanced lever system to step up capsule deflection by a factor of 80 to 100.
4. **Install Clockwork Drum:** Mount a vertically oriented brass cylinder containing an 8-day mechanical spring-driven clockwork mechanism with an escapement governor.
5. **Fabricate Stylus and Nib:** Attach a lightweight stylus with a miniature V-shaped brass ink reservoir and capillary feed to the primary indicator arm.
6. **Calibrate and Chart:** Wrap a paper chart printed with millibar/hectopascal scales and hourly grids around the drum, fill the nib with glycerin-based non-drying ink, and zero the pen against a calibrated mercury barometer.

## Materials needed

- **Essential:** Spring **[Steel](/en/materials/steel)** for internal counterbalance springs, sheet bronze or **[Brass](/en/materials/brass)** for vacuum capsules and mechanical levers, heavy **[Paper](/en/materials/paper)** for printed barograms, slow-drying **[Ink](/en/materials/ink)** (water, dye, and glycerin mixture).
- **Tools:** Sheet metal stamps, vacuum pump, soldering torch, watchmaker's lathe, jeweler's files, calibrated reference barometer.
- **Substitutes:** A floating siphon mercury barometer with a light counterweight-and-pulley linkage can be used in place of aneroid vacuum capsules.

## Variants and improvements

- **Aneroid Barograph:** The standard portable design utilizing a vertical column of stacked aneroid chambers.
- **Mercury Barograph:** A laboratory station instrument where a float on the open cistern of a large mercury column moves the pen lever directly.
- **Microbarograph:** Features oversized bellows and high amplification ratios to detect minute atmospheric acoustic waves, explosion shocks, and volcanic disturbances.
- **Seven-Day Marine Barograph:** Features damped oil dashpots to insulate the recording needle from ship vibration and rolling waves.

## Limits and risks

- **Hysteresis and Metal Fatigue:** Continual flexing of thin metallic diaphragms causes mechanical elastic lag (hysteresis) over long periods.
- **Pen Friction:** Stylus friction against chart paper can impede needle movement unless dampened with periodic gentle mechanical vibration or high leverage.
- **Inking Failure:** Low humidity can dry the ink reservoir while freezing temperatures can cause water-based inks to solidify without adequate glycerin addition.
