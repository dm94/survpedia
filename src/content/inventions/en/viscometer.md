# Brief description

A capillary viscometer (frequently referred to as an Ostwald or Ubbelohde viscometer) is a precision laboratory instrument used to determine the kinematic viscosity of liquids by measuring the time required for a fixed volume of fluid to flow under gravity through a calibrated glass capillary tube. In an industrial or rebuilding context, it is indispensable for quality control of motor oils, hydraulic fluids, biofuels, and chemical syntheses.

## Use / Function

- **Lubricant Grading:** Verifying the viscosity index and lubricating performance of petroleum motor oils, vegetable lubricants, and greases.
- **Fuel Quality Assessment:** Ensuring correct flow characteristics of biofuels, heavy fuel oils, and kerosene before engine injection.
- **Chemical Analysis:** Monitoring fluid consistency in sugar refining, polymer concentration, fermentation broths, and resin processing.

## Operating principle

1. **Poiseuille's Law of Laminar Flow:** The volumetric flow rate of an incompressible fluid flowing through a narrow circular pipe under laminar conditions is inversely proportional to its dynamic viscosity ($\eta$) and directly proportional to the hydrostatic pressure head and tube radius to the fourth power ($Q = \frac{\pi \Delta P r^4}{8 \eta L}$).
2. **Fixed Fluid Column:** A known volume of liquid held between two etched fiducial marks drains under its own gravitational weight through a very narrow capillary.
3. **Efflux Time Measurement:** The time taken ($t$) for the liquid meniscus to drop between the upper and lower marks is directly proportional to the kinematic viscosity ($\nu = \eta / \rho = C \cdot t$), where $C$ is a calibration constant determined using a reference fluid like pure water.

## How to create it

1. **Blow Glass Reservoir and Capillary Tube:** Take borosilicate glass tubing and form a U-shaped apparatus featuring a lower wide reservoir bulb on one arm and a narrow, uniform capillary bore (typically 0.5 to 1.5 mm diameter) on the other.
2. **Form Timing Bulb:** Above the capillary on the measuring arm, blow a small upper timing bulb (approximately 5 to 10 mL volume).
3. **Etch Fiducial Marks:** Using an abrasive glass cutter or acid paste, scratch two permanent horizontal reference lines: mark $A$ immediately above the timing bulb and mark $B$ directly below it (above the top of the capillary).
4. **Attach Flexible Suction Tube:** Fit a clean piece of rubber tubing to the measuring arm to allow drawing liquid up into the timing bulb by mouth or bulb suction.
5. **Set Up Water Bath:** Mount the viscometer vertically inside a constant-temperature transparent water bath, as liquid viscosity varies exponentially with temperature.
6. **Calibrate with Distilled Water:** Charge the instrument with pure distilled water at 20°C, measure the efflux time across several trials, and calculate the instrument calibration constant ($C = \nu_{\text{water}} / t_{\text{water}}$).

## Materials needed

- **Essential:** Borosilicate or laboratory **[Glass](/en/materials/glass)** tubing, pure distilled **[Water](/en/materials/water)** for calibration and bath, flexible **[Natural Rubber](/en/materials/natural-rubber)** tubing.
- **Tools:** Glassblowing torch, calipers, accurate stopwatch, precision thermometer.
- **Substitutes:** Clear plastic tubing with a precision metal orifice can serve for rough workshop viscosity comparisons (such as a Ford cup).

## Variants and improvements

- **Ostwald Viscometer:** The classic U-tube design; simple to fabricate but requires identical filling volumes for every test to maintain a constant hydrostatic head.
- **Ubbelohde Suspended-Level Viscometer:** Incorporates a third vented side tube that decouples the bottom of the capillary from the lower reservoir, making efflux time completely independent of sample filling volume.
- **Falling Ball Viscometer (Hoppler):** Measures the terminal sinking velocity of a calibrated steel ball through a fluid-filled inclined glass tube.

## Limits and risks

- **Particulate Blockage:** Suspended lint, dust, or carbon particles in un-filtered fluids can easily clog the capillary bore and distort efflux times.
- **Turbulence Breakdown:** If the capillary diameter is too large or the liquid too thin, Reynolds numbers exceed laminar limits, invalidating Poiseuille's law.
- **Extreme Temperature Sensitivity:** Fluid viscosity typically changes by 1% to 5% per degree Celsius; failure to maintain thermal equilibrium will cause massive reading errors.
