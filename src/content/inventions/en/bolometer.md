# Brief description

A bolometer is a sensitive thermal radiation detector that measures the power of incident electromagnetic radiation—particularly infrared light—by recording the temperature-dependent change in electrical resistance of a fine blackened metallic absorber. Invented in 1878 by American astronomer Samuel Pierpont Langley, it provided early science with an unprecedented tool to map the solar infrared spectrum and quantify radiant heat.

## Use / Function

- **Thermal Radiation Detection:** Measuring minuscule quantities of infrared, microwave, or submillimeter radiation from distant thermal sources.
- **Spectrometry:** Mapping absorption and emission spectra beyond the visible range, including infrared solar bands.
- **Astronomy:** Measuring the heat flux and surface temperatures of celestial objects such as the Moon, planets, and bright stars.

## Operating principle

1. **Radiation Absorption:** Incident radiant energy strikes a blackened metal strip (such as platinum foil coated with lampblack), which absorbs photons efficiently and converts their energy into heat.
2. **Thermal Resistance Change:** As the strip warms, its electrical resistance increases proportionally according to its positive temperature coefficient of resistance ($\Delta R = R_0 \cdot \alpha \cdot \Delta T$).
3. **Bridge Unbalance:** The absorbing strip forms one arm of a balanced Wheatstone bridge circuit. A matching unexposed shielded strip forms an adjacent arm to compensate for ambient temperature drift.
4. **Current Deflection:** Any temperature rise induced by the incoming radiation unbalances the bridge, driving an electric current through a sensitive galvanometer whose deflection quantifies radiant flux.

## How to create it

1. **Roll Thin Metal Strips:** Hammer or roll platinum or nickel foil into two paper-thin, identical ribbons roughly 1 cm long and less than 0.01 mm thick to minimize thermal heat capacity.
2. **Apply Absorbing Coating:** Coat the front-facing surface of one strip with a microscopic layer of lampblack or platinum black soot from a smoky hydrocarbon flame to maximize radiation absorptivity.
3. **Construct Support Chamber:** Mount the two ribbons side-by-side inside an evacuated or shielded glass housing, exposing only the blackened strip to an optical aperture while keeping the reference strip shielded.
4. **Wire Wheatstone Bridge:** Connect the two ribbons as adjacent arms of a Wheatstone bridge alongside two precision balance resistors made of low-temperature-coefficient copper or manganin wire.
5. **Connect Detection Galvanometer:** Place a mirror galvanometer across the detection nodes and connect a stable low-voltage battery or voltaic pile to power the bridge.
6. **Balance and Calibrate:** Adjust balancing resistors with the radiation aperture closed until galvanometer deflection is zero, then calibrate against a known thermal emitter (such as a blackbody cavity at boiling water temperature).

## Materials needed

- **Essential:** Ultra-thin **[Platinum](/en/materials/platinum)** or nickel foil, insulated **[Wire](/en/materials/wire)**, **[Copper](/en/materials/copper)** connecting leads and balancing coils, clear **[Glass](/en/materials/glass)** casing or evacuation bulb.
- **Tools:** Rolling mill or jeweler's anvil, soldering kit, sensitive galvanometer, precision resistance box.
- **Substitutes:** Thin iron or copper foil can be used instead of platinum in rudimentary setups, though with greater drift and lower sensitivity.

## Variants and improvements

- **Langley Platinum Bolometer:** The classic two-strip platinum ribbon design capable of detecting temperature fluctuations as small as one hundred-thousandth of a degree Celsius.
- **Thermistor Bolometer:** Uses high negative temperature coefficient semiconductor metal oxides for greater resistance change per degree.
- **Cryogenic / Superconducting Bolometer:** Modern astronomical instruments cooled near absolute zero, operating on the razor-sharp resistance transition edge of superconductors to detect single infrared and submillimeter photons.

## Limits and risks

- **Ambient Temperature Drift:** Ambient room temperature fluctuations can cause resistance changes larger than the radiation signal unless differential compensation is meticulously maintained.
- **Drafts and Convection:** Stray air currents strip heat from the ribbon, demanding operation inside an evacuated glass vessel or baffling tube.
- **Fragility:** The gossamer-thin metallic foils are exceptionally fragile and easily severed by physical vibration or accidental electrical overload.
