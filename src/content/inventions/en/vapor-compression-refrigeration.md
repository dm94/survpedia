# Brief description

Vapor-compression refrigeration is a thermodynamic cooling system that uses a circulating liquid refrigerant to absorb heat from an enclosed space and reject it to the outside environment through continuous evaporation and condensation cycles.

## Use / Function

- **Food and Produce Preservation**: Prevents bacterial decay and extends the shelf life of perishable food, meat, and dairy products.
- **Medical and Vaccine Cold Chain**: Preserves vaccines, blood supplies, and temperature-sensitive pharmaceuticals.
- **Air Conditioning**: Provides thermal comfort and humidity control in residential, industrial, and medical buildings.
- **Industrial Processing**: Cools chemical reactors, food processing plants, and ice manufacturing facilities.

## Operating principle

Vapor-compression refrigeration operates on a closed mechanical thermodynamic loop utilizing phase changes of a volatile refrigerant fluid:

1. **Evaporation**: Low-pressure liquid refrigerant absorbs heat from the cold refrigerated space inside evaporator coils, boiling into a low-pressure gas.
2. **Compression**: A mechanical compressor squeezes the gas refrigerant, raising its pressure and temperature significantly.
3. **Condensation**: The hot, high-pressure gas passes through condenser coils outside the cold space, releasing heat to the ambient air and condensing back into a high-pressure liquid.
4. **Expansion**: The liquid refrigerant passes through a narrow expansion valve or capillary tube, dropping its pressure and temperature before re-entering the evaporator coils.

## How to create it

1. **Construct Coils**: Form evaporator and condenser coil loops from high-thermal-conductivity **[Copper](/en/materials/copper)** tubing.
2. **Build or Adapt a Compressor**: Assemble a piston or rotary gas compressor driven by a steam engine, water wheel, or electric motor to compress the refrigerant vapor.
3. **Install Expansion Device**: Fit a narrow metering expansion orifice or adjustable needle valve between the high-pressure condenser exit and low-pressure evaporator inlet.
4. **Charge with Refrigerant**: Evacuate air from the closed loop and charge the system with a volatile refrigerant such as **[Ether](/en/materials/ether)**, ammonia, or hydrocarbon gas.

## Materials needed

- **Tubing and Fittings**: High-pressure **[Copper](/en/materials/copper)** or **[Brass](/en/materials/brass)** tubing and leak-tight connectors.
- **Structural Frame**: **[Steel](/en/materials/steel)** or iron casing for mounting the compressor and heat exchanger assemblies.
- **Refrigerant Fluid**: **[Ether](/en/materials/ether)**, anhydrous ammonia, sulfur dioxide, or propane.
- **Insulation Material**: Mineral wool, cork, or polyurethane foam to isolate the refrigerated box.

## Variants and improvements

- **Early Ether Machines**: Compression systems built by James Harrison and Ferdinand Carré using volatile ethyl ether or ammonia for artificial ice making.
- **Hermetic Sealed Units**: Modern refrigerators with electric motors and compressors sealed inside welded steel shells to eliminate refrigerant leakage.
- **Multi-Stage Compression**: Uses intermediate cooling between multiple compression stages for ultra-low temperature industrial freezing.

## Limits and risks

- **Refrigerant Toxicity and Flammability**: Early refrigerants like ammonia and sulfur dioxide are highly toxic; ether and propane are flammable and explosive upon leakage.
- **High Mechanical Wear**: Continuous mechanical operation of compressors requires reliable lubrication and tight machining tolerances.
- **Thermal Losses**: Poor cabinet insulation or leaky door seals lead to excessive energy consumption and rapid thermal rise.
