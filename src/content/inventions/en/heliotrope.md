# Brief description

A heliotrope is a geodetic surveying instrument that uses an adjustable plane mirror to reflect a concentrated beam of sunlight directly toward a distant observation station. Invented in 1821 by Carl Friedrich Gauss for the triangulation survey of the Kingdom of Hanover, the heliotrope revolutionized long-range terrestrial cartography. It produced a luminous point of light so piercing that it could be sighted through surveyor telescopes over distances exceeding 50 to 100 kilometers across haze, valleys, and mountain peaks, far surpassing torches, flags, or signal fires.

## Use / Function

- **Primary Triangulation:** Establishing high-precision geodetic control networks, baseline measurements, and national boundary surveys across long baselines.
- **Topographic Surveying:** Marking distant summits, church steeples, or observation towers as unmistakable optical targets for theodolites.
- **Optical Semaphore Signaling:** Transmitting telegraphic and military messages across line-of-sight distances via controlled flashes of Morse or alphanumeric codes.
- **Scale:** Geodetic and territorial cartography, national defense surveying, and civil engineering infrastructure.

## Operating principle

1. **Specular Reflection of the Solar Disc:** A flat silvered mirror acts as a planar reflector, casting a cone of sunlight whose angular divergence is roughly equal to the angular diameter of the sun (approximately 32 arcminutes).
2. **Long-Distance Beam Concentration:** Over terrestrial distances of 30 to 70 km, this narrow cone of light forms a tiny, intensely bright spot that appears through a telescope as an artificial star of remarkable clarity.
3. **Collimation Alignment:** To direct the light beam accurately toward a distant point invisible to the naked eye, the heliotrope incorporates a dual-axis sighting telescope or an auxiliary alignment tube with crosshairs.
4. **Heliostatic Solar Tracking:** Because the Earth rotates (causing the sun to move across the sky at approximately 15 degrees per hour), the operator periodically adjusts fine tangent screws on the mirror mounting to keep the reflected beam centered on the remote observer.

## How to create it

1. **Construct the Tripod Base:** Build a rigid wooden or metal mounting platform that can be firmly leveled and anchored over a survey monument or benchmark.
2. **Install Sighting Telescope:** Fix a low-magnification brass telescope with crosshairs onto the base, aligning it precisely with the distant survey station.
3. **Mount Gimbaled Mirror:** Position a high-grade flat optical mirror (typically 5 to 10 cm in diameter) on a two-axis swivel bracket above or alongside the telescope line of sight.
4. **Integrate Aiming Ring:** Mount an auxiliary aiming ring with crosshairs or a small collimating aperture in front of the mirror, precisely centered on the optical axis of the sighting telescope.
5. **Add Fine Tangent Screws:** Fit fine-threaded thumb screws to allow smooth, micro-adjustable altitude and azimuth rotation of the mirror.
6. **Operating the Instrument:** Look through the telescope to center the crosshairs on the target station, then manipulate the mirror until the reflected sunlight passes cleanly through the aiming ring directly down the target vector.

## Materials needed

- **Essential:** High-reflectivity flat silvered **[Glass](/en/materials/glass)** mirror; turned or cast **[Metal](/en/materials/metal)** (brass or steel) for gimbals, thumb screws, and telescope mounts; seasoned **[Wood](/en/materials/wood)** for the tripod and transport case.
- **Tools:** Lathe for screw-cutting and tube machining, optical collimator or spirit level, jeweler's files, drill press.
- **Substitutes:** A polished speculum metal plate or flat stainless steel sheet can substitute for silvered glass; a simple wooden tube with wire crosshairs can serve as the sighting guide.

## Variants and improvements

- **Gauss Heliotrope:** The original design featuring an integrated telescope with internal silvered reference prisms to verify beam alignment simultaneously with sighting.
- **Steinheil Heliotrope:** A compact, pocket-sized instrument employing a small mirror with an un-silvered central spot that produces an optical image of the sun coincident with the target.
- **Surveyor Flash Heliotrope:** Simplified handheld model mounted on standard theodolite tripods for routine secondary triangulation.
- **Heliograph:** Modified heliotrope equipped with a mechanical shutter or key-actuated tilting mechanism to transmit Morse code signals.

## Limits and risks

- **Total Solar Dependence:** Completely inoperative on overcast days, in dense cloud cover, during precipitation, or at night (requiring Drummond limelight or carbide beacons as nocturnal alternatives).
- **Beam Drift:** Earth's diurnal rotation causes the reflected beam to drift off target within a couple of minutes if not constantly adjusted by an operator.
- **Eye Safety:** Never look directly into the primary reflected beam from close range, as the concentrated solar rays can cause retinal burns.
